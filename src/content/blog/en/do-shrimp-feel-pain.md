We often picture shrimp as tiny things with no inner life. Science says otherwise.

## What does "sentient" mean?

A sentient being can feel: pain, fear, stress, and probably wellbeing too. The question isn't whether shrimp are "smart", but whether what happens to them matters to them.

## What the studies show

Researchers can't ask a shrimp whether it hurts. Instead they look for **signs** that, taken together, separate pain from a simple reflex.

- **Nociceptors.** Like us, shrimp have nerve cells that detect what could injure them. That's the biological basis pain needs.
- **Targeted protective behaviour.** In 2008, a team at Queen's University Belfast applied an irritant to one antenna of prawns (*Palaemon elegans*). The prawns rubbed and groomed that specific antenna for a long time. When a local anaesthetic was applied, the behaviour decreased. A reflex doesn't respond to painkillers; pain does.
- **Learning and trade-offs.** In decapods, animals learn to avoid places where they were hurt, and they weigh costs and benefits: putting up with discomfort to keep a resource, or giving it up when it hurts too much.

## The report that changed UK law

In 2021, the UK government asked researchers at the London School of Economics, led by philosopher Jonathan Birch, to look into the question. After reviewing more than 300 studies, they concluded there is **strong evidence of sentience** in decapod crustaceans and in cephalopods (octopuses, squid). In 2022, the UK included them in its animal sentience law.

The evidence is richer for some decapods, like crabs, than for shrimp, simply because shrimp have been studied less. The report therefore recommends protecting **all** decapods: leaving shrimp out for lack of studies would make no sense.

## The benefit of the doubt

There are still open questions about exactly what a shrimp experiences. But with hundreds of billions of animals every year ([here are the numbers](../why-shrimp/)), waiting for absolute certainty would mean looking away. When we don't know everything, we protect: that's the precautionary principle.
